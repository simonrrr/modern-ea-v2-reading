Paul R. Lawrence & Jay W. Lorsch (1967). New management job: the integrator. Harvard Business Review, 45(6), 142–151.

  • Autonomy and coherence pull against each other
  • Integrators win through expertise, not authority
  • Integrators translate between floors
  • Confront problems; keep force in reserve

A study of ten organisations in three industries. Firms need more specialisation (differentiation) and tighter coordination (integration), but “these two needs are essentially antagonistic, and one can usually be achieved only at the expense of the other.” Effective integrators help firms get both. They are influential “because of their knowledge and expertise”, not “the formal authority of their positions”, and they “speak the language of each of the specialist groups”. The firms that integrated best mostly settled conflict by confrontation, “placing all relevant facts before the disputants”. They also used some forcing as a backup, while smoothing over differences meant “nothing happens”.

Caveat The “happy as larks” words are a manager’s, which the authors quote. The original reads “In the more effective, the integrators…”: the slide adds “[organizations]”. The online text has no print page numbers.

Chris Atkinson & Luke Harland (October 2026). Guardrails, not Gatekeepers: Atom Bank’s Journey to Conversational Governance. FastFlowConf 2026 (video).

  • Most decisions are hats or haircuts; a few are tattoos

Frederick P. Brooks, Jr. (1986). No Silver Bullet: Essence and Accidents of Software Engineering. Information Processing 86 (IFIP Tenth World Computing Conference), 1069–1076. Reprinted in Computer, 20(4), 10–19 (1987).

Martin Fowler (2004; rewritten 22 August 2024). Strangler Fig Application. martinfowler.com (bliki).

  • Grow the new around the old

Strangler figs in Queensland grow around a host tree until the tree dies and the fig stands in its shape. Fowler’s name for replacing a legacy system gradually, by growing the new one around it.

Caveat The slide shows the pattern; this is where the name comes from. The 2004 post was called “Strangler Application”; Fowler renamed it to bring back the fig.

Simon Wardley (2016–2018). Doctrine. Chapter 4 of Wardley Maps: Topographical intelligence in business. Medium.

  • Doctrine: universally useful patterns

Doctrine is the set of principles that apply whatever your landscape or context: focus on user needs, use a common language, challenge assumptions; about forty in all, grouped into four phases. Wardley calls it not right but “consistently useful for the time being”.

Caveat The slide’s table is credited to a variation by Steve Purkis. Margaret-Anne Storey (2026). From Technical Debt to Cognitive and Intent Debt: Rethinking Software Health in the Age of AI. arXiv:2603.22106.

  • AI piles up three kinds of debt, faster

A Triple Debt Model: technical debt in the code, cognitive debt in people (lost shared understanding), and intent debt in what is written down (missing rationale, goals and constraints, for people and for agents). “Generative AI is accelerating software development”, and code arrives faster than teams can understand it.

Caveat Cited as version 4 of the preprint (6 April 2026). The slide shows the paper’s own figure.

  • Three ways to maintain a boat

Three sailors in the 1968 Golden Globe race: “at heart, it was a contest of maintenance styles.” Knox-Johnston’s style was “Whatever comes, deal with it.” Crowhurst’s was “Hope for the best.” It killed him. Moitessier told Brand: “My rule is, a new boat every day.”
